MaRiO: Multi-agent Collaborative Reasoning via Shared Observations in MLLMs

Abstract

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) show strong visual perception capabilities, but existing evaluations largely focus on single-view or multi-view settings with shared camera parameters, leaving the challenge of multi-agent collaborative scenarios underexplored. In such settings, multiple agents observe a shared environment from independent viewpoints, and reliable decisions require reasoning across these independent observations of the same environment. To study this problem, we introduce MaRiO, a benchmark for evaluating multi-agent collaborative reasoning, and evaluate 23 state-of-the-art MLLMs on it. Our analysis reveals three key findings: (1) tasks governed by static perception are generally easier than tasks that require transformational event sequences; (2) most models struggle to resolve cross-agent correspondences, with errors increasing with scene complexity; and (3) models show limited ability to leverage geometric cues present in the scene. As an initial step toward addressing these challenges, we explore geometry-guided synthetic view augmentation that generates intermediate transition frames between agent observations, providing auxiliary spatial context that improves performance in certain categories. Overall, our results highlight that multi-agent collaborative reasoning remains an open challenge and an important direction for multimodal embodied systems.

Motivation

Single view
One image, so there are no frames to match.
Multi-view
Several images, captured along one trajectory and usually shipped with poses, intrinsics or point clouds.
Multi-agent
Each agent has its own egocentric frame and no shared metadata. Matching the frames is the first half of every question.

The MaRiO benchmark

3,255questions
1,680observation sets
1,371agent pairs
309agent triplets
5tasks
23models

Input. Two or three synchronized views and a four-choice question. No camera poses, no intrinsics, no depth, no scene graphs.

Scenes. 913 real image sets from RealEstate10K and NAVI, plus 767 synthetic sets from VirtualHome, Habitat-Sim and AI2-THOR.

Distance Awareness, Occlusion and Relative Position ask for one spatial fact once the views are matched. Egocentric Motion and Navigation ask the model to compose a transformation between frames.

Three domain-expert annotators drafted and reviewed every question, and a further annotator cross-verified them. An LLM polished phrasing and drafted distractors. Every ground-truth label is human.

Results

The gap. Humans score 73.2. The best model, Gemini-2.5-Pro, stops at 55.7, which is 17.5 points short. The best open model, Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct, gets 42.0. Chance is 23.9.

By task. Navigation and Egocentric Motion sit closest to chance. Distance Awareness and Occlusion hold up best. Models still see the scene. What they cannot do is carry a direction out of one agent's frame and into another's.

Analysis

From static perception to compositional reasoning

Relationship between the two groups

Pooled over every model, compositional accuracy barely tracks static perceptual accuracy. The fit gives a slope of 0.15 and an R2 of 0.028, and per-model regressions are just as flat, so strength on one group says little about the other.

Effect of removing observations

Withholding one observation, or all of them, leaves open-source accuracy on the compositional tasks almost unchanged, and several models stay above chance there with no image at all. Proprietary models degrade consistently, and Distance Awareness collapses for everyone once the views are gone.

TakeawayStrong static perception does not translate into reliable geometric reasoning in MLLMs because they fail to integrate distributed multi-agent collaborative observations into single unified representations.

Cross-agent correspondence under relational complexity

Increasing the number of agents

Going from two agents to three lowers accuracy across almost every model and category. Each extra viewpoint enlarges the set of correspondences that must hold at once, and Navigation and Egocentric Motion are affected most.

Shared environmental landmarks

Anchors are static objects visible to more than one agent, the natural reference points for aligning views. Accuracy climbs with anchor density to the six-to-nine bin and falls away past ten, where many similar candidates make a reference ambiguous rather than useful. The fall is steeper with three agents.

TakeawayAs independent observations from multiple agents and scene clutter increase, MLLMs struggle to resolve cross-view correspondences, leading to unstable reasoning.

Geometric consistency and structural cues

Orientation consistency under reflection

Mirroring the images and swapping left for right in the text leaves a geometrically identical question. Between 22 and 32 percent of answers change regardless, with bias ratios of 0.50 to 0.59 for the models shown. Directional questions change more often than obstacle questions, which points at the direction words rather than the pixels.

Depth cue complexity

Stronger depth structure does not improve performance. Most models do worse where depth edges are denser, and the largest drop is in simple scenes. Depth is estimated afterwards and is never shown to the model.

TakeawayMLLMs exhibit weak use of geometric cues, with predictions sensitive to orientation changes and largely unaffected by scene geometry, leading to inconsistent reasoning and poor cross-agent alignment.

Human reasoning difficulty

Human errors and the human-model comparison

Human evaluators reach 73.2 percent. Auditing every question at least one evaluator missed, direction confusion dominates: 13 of the 23 incorrect responses involve a rotated, mirrored, reversed or reordered direction. On the same 81 questions Gemini-2.5-Pro missed 19 of the 60 that every evaluator answered correctly, and answered 11 of the 21 that tripped at least one person.

TakeawayHuman errors reflect the cognitive cost of composing viewpoint transformations and do not coincide with model errors, indicating that MaRiO’s questions are answerable while remaining demanding for both humans and MLLMs.

Geometry-guided view augmentation

Geometry-guided view synthesis, in four stages.

Setup. A diffusion video model turns the rendered in-between frames into novel views. They go in as extra context for the same question, and no new agent is added.

Effect. Qwen2.5-VL-7B gains 1.1 overall, with 4.5 on Occlusion and 3.9 on Relative Position. Spatially trained models gain nothing or lose ground, which the paper reads as the generated frames introducing small geometric inconsistencies. Twelve frames is the best setting, and cropping real frames instead does not reproduce the gain, so what helps is the new viewpoint and not the extra pixels.
