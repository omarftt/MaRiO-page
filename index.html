MaRiO

Multi-agent Collaborative Reasoning via Shared Observations in MLLMs

Several agents see the same room from independent viewpoints — no shared camera poses, no shared coordinate frame, just RGB. Before a model can reason about space, it has to work out how the views relate.

Abstract

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) show strong visual perception capabilities, but existing evaluations largely focus on single-view or multi-view settings with shared camera parameters, leaving the challenge of multi-agent collaborative scenarios underexplored. In such settings, multiple agents observe a shared environment from independent viewpoints, and reliable decisions require reasoning across these independent observations of the same environment. To study this problem, we introduce MaRiO, a benchmark for evaluating multi-agent collaborative reasoning, and evaluate 23 state-of-the-art MLLMs on it. Our analysis reveals three key findings: (1) tasks governed by static perception are generally easier than tasks that require transformational event sequences; (2) most models struggle to resolve cross-agent correspondences, with errors increasing with scene complexity; and (3) models show limited ability to leverage geometric cues present in the scene. As an initial step toward addressing these challenges, we explore geometry-guided synthetic view augmentation that generates intermediate transition frames between agent observations, providing auxiliary spatial context that improves performance in certain categories. Overall, our results highlight that multi-agent collaborative reasoning remains an open challenge and an important direction for multimodal embodied systems.

The gap

Motivation

Prior benchmarks hand the model one coordinate frame. Collaborative agents don't have one.

Single-view benchmarks are limited to one perspective and cannot resolve cross-view ambiguities. Multi-view benchmarks add images, but they are typically captured along a shared trajectory whose geometry is known or recoverable, and they usually ship camera poses, intrinsics, 3D coordinates or point clouds alongside the pixels.

Multi-agent settings remove that scaffolding. Each agent observes the environment from its own egocentric frame with no shared geometric metadata, so a model must first establish correspondences between independent viewpoints and align them, and only then infer spatial and geometric relationships. That two-stage requirement — correspondence, then reasoning — is what MaRiO isolates.

Every question is four-choice multiple choice with a randomized option order. Each one is built on a set of synchronized views of a shared scene, captured by two or three independently oriented agents. Models receive the raw images and the question text — nothing else. No camera poses, no intrinsics, no depth, no point clouds, no scene graphs.

Scenes come from both real-world sources and simulators, to balance realism against control. 200 scenes from RealEstate10K and 35 from NAVI contribute 913 real image sets; 39 scenes built in VirtualHome, Habitat-Sim and AI2-THOR contribute 767 synthetic ones, some augmented with lighting variation, object perturbation or lens artifacts.

Five tasks, two groups

What the questions ask

Three tasks are answerable from what the views show; two require composing transformations between them.

Static Perception covers spatial relationships and visibility that can be read off the agents' views once they are aligned: Distance Awareness (qualitative depth and proximity from an egocentric viewpoint), Occlusion / Visibility (viewpoint-dependent visibility and object enumeration under partial occlusion), and Relative Position (relative agent positions and orientations).

Compositional Reasoning over Geometric Transformations covers tasks that require sequencing transformations: Egocentric Motion (orientation under a change of reference frame) and Navigation (sequential state updates composed from translations and rotations).

The grouping is not stylistic. Across the 19 open-source models, task-wise accuracies within Static Perception correlate strongly with each other (Spearman ρ = 0.855–0.933), while Egocentric Motion and Navigation correlate only weakly with those tasks and with each other (ρ = 0.407).

How it was built

Construction

Questions were written and cross-verified by three expert annotators; the LLM only polished phrasing and drafted distractors.

Each reasoning category has a written specification fixing its spatial primitives, reference-frame assumptions and reasoning constraints. Three domain-expert annotators drafted candidate questions against those specifications, ensuring each one requires evidence from more than one agent's view. Every draft was reviewed by at least one other annotator for spatial validity, ambiguity and consistency; flagged questions were reformulated over the same observation set where possible and discarded otherwise, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

An LLM assisted in two narrow ways: polishing the grammar and phrasing of human-authored questions, and proposing three plausible distractors per question. Every output was reviewed, edited, accepted or rejected by a human annotator. Ground-truth labels were authored by humans only; no LLM output was accepted as a correct answer.

To check that the three annotators produced a consistent benchmark, 375 items (25 per annotator per category) were audited by an LLM judge with annotator identity hidden, against four binary criteria. Pass rates were 94.9% pooled and 92.8–98.8% per annotator, highest for answer-option consistency (97.3%) and entity concreteness (97.1%), lowest for overall style match (91.2%).

Results

Zero-shot performance

Every model we evaluated falls well short of human accuracy.

Human evaluators reach 73.2%. The best model, Gemini-2.5-Pro, reaches 55.7%; the best open-source model, Ovis2.5-9B, reaches 41.2%, and most others cluster between 30–42% against a random baseline of 23.9%. The largest difficulties appear in the compositional tasks — Navigation and Egocentric Motion — where many models approach near-random accuracy, while Occlusion and Relative Position hold up comparatively well.

Analysis

What goes wrong

The bottleneck is cross-agent correspondence, not perception.

01 Static perception does not carry over to compositional reasoning.

If being good at reading a scene implied being good at composing transformations across scenes, the two should track each other. They barely do. Pooling every model, the regression of compositional accuracy on static perceptual accuracy has a slope of 0.15 and an R2 of 0.028; per-model regressions give consistently low R2 values, and the sign of the slope is not even stable across models.

So while models can reconcile static spatial relationships from multiple egocentric observations, that ability does not translate into reliably resolving sequences of geometric transformations.

02 Many open-source models barely use the other agent's view.

Withholding one randomly selected observation, or all of them, while leaving the question unchanged separates models that integrate distributed evidence from models that do not. Proprietary models degrade consistently across every reasoning category when a viewpoint is removed — their predictions genuinely depend on information spread across observations.

Open-source models show only minor changes, and in some categories none at all. On compositional tasks several of them stay above random with no visual input whatsoever, which means those answers are coming from answer priors and option bias rather than from cross-agent visual evidence.

03 A third agent makes it worse.

Going from two agents to three adds viewpoints — and more information ought to help. Instead accuracy declines across nearly all models and categories, because each extra agent enlarges the set of cross-agent correspondences that have to be resolved simultaneously. The largest drops land on Navigation and Egocentric Motion, which already have the lowest baselines.

04 Shared landmarks help, until the scene gets cluttered.

Shared anchors — static objects visible to more than one agent — are the natural reference points for aligning views, so you would expect more of them to be strictly better. The relationship is non-monotonic instead: accuracy improves as anchor density rises from very sparse scenes up to the [6, 9] range, then degrades past 10.

Past that point the landmarks stop disambiguating and start competing: many similar candidates make matching a spatial reference across agents ambiguous. Navigation and Egocentric Motion are the most sensitive, and the effect is sharper with three agents than with two.

05 Mirror the scene, swap the words, and the answer flips.

Flipping the images horizontally and swapping the directional language to match (“turn left” → “turn right”) produces a geometrically equivalent question. A model with a stable spatial representation should answer both identically. Across models, 22–32% of questions change outcome, and bias ratios of 0.47–0.59 — where 0.50 means perfectly symmetric fragility — show that no model is robustly orientation-invariant.

Splitting Navigation isolates the cause. Directional questions, where both the image and the text are transformed, drop by a mean of +2.6 pp; obstacle questions, where only the image is flipped, drop by +0.4 pp. The instability is driven by directional language cues rather than by the geometry — and the same pattern holds across all 15 open-source models in the supplementary.

06 Models do not exploit the geometry that is already in the image.

If models read geometric structure from pixels, scenes with stronger monocular depth cues should be easier. Grouping samples by the number of distinct depth layers and comparing high against low depth-edge density within each group shows the opposite or nothing at all: most models perform worse at higher edge density, with the largest degradation in simple scenes, and effects that are small and inconsistent in moderate and complex ones.

Across 20 models and three complexity strata, only one comparison survives Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0025), and no model shows a significant positive effect. Additional geometric structure is being treated as visual clutter rather than as useful spatial information.

07 Humans and models fail on different questions.

Humans do not find MaRiO trivial either — 73.2% overall, with the lowest scores on Egocentric Motion and Navigation. Auditing every question at least one evaluator missed, direction confusion dominates: 13 of 23 incorrect responses involve a rotated, mirrored, reversed or reordered direction. That is the signature of mental-rotation cost, which cognitive science predicts should scale with the degree of perspective change, and it concentrates exactly where the paper predicts — three-agent Egocentric Motion.

The two error sets barely overlap. Of the 60 questions every evaluator answered correctly, Gemini-2.5-Pro failed 19. Of the 21 questions with at least one human error, it answered 11 correctly. Human success does not track model success, which is the evidence that MaRiO's questions are genuinely answerable rather than simply hard for everyone.

An initial step

Geometry-guided view augmentation

Synthesizing the views between agents helps general models on cross-view tasks, but does not close the gap.

If the failures come from not being able to relate independent viewpoints, then showing the model what lies between them should help. We reconstruct a 3D point cloud of the scene from the agents' observations, interpolate camera poses between them, and render intermediate views from that geometry. Those renders then guide a diffusion-based video generator, which produces a sequence of synthetic frames revealing how the independent observations relate spatially.

The generated frames are supplied as auxiliary visual context, not as an extra agent: video-capable models receive the video alongside the original images, image-only models receive sampled frames appended to them, and the prompt states explicitly that these are supporting context.

The gains are category-specific rather than uniform. General MLLMs benefit most, particularly on Navigation and Relative Position — Qwen2.5-VL-7B gains +1.1 overall, with +3.9 on Relative Position and +4.5 on Occlusion. Spatial reasoning models benefit little or degrade: they already try to infer geometric structure from the inputs, and diffusion-generated views introduce small geometric inconsistencies that disturb precise metric reasoning.

Ablations confirm where the gain comes from. Twelve sampled frames works best; permuting the interpolation order changes little; and replacing the synthesized views with cropped frames from the original images does not reproduce the improvement. The benefit comes from the novel viewpoints, not from having more frames.

The i-th image is the observation of agent i; that mapping is stated in the prompt rather than inferred from image order, and it is reinforced with inline textual references for interfaces that support interleaved image–text input.

Source assets keep their own licences: RealEstate10K (per that project's redistribution terms), NAVI (CC BY 4.0), VirtualHome (MIT), Habitat-Sim (MIT, with bundled scenes carrying separate licences) and AI2-THOR (Apache 2.0).
